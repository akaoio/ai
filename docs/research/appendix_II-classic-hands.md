# Appendix II — Classic Hands

---

## Why This Appendix Exists

The nine core modules of this knowledge base teach poker as a system of logic: pot odds, equity, EV, GTO balance. That system is correct. But it is incomplete as a description of how poker is actually played between human beings.

Humans do not play GTO poker. They play poker through the filter of their psychology — their ego, their fear, their boredom, their tilt, their need to prove something. A hand that is mathematically correct to fold becomes a call because the player cannot accept being bluffed. A hand that is correct to bet becomes a check because the player is afraid of losing what they have. A player on a losing streak shoves far too wide because they are chasing losses. A player who just won a big pot calls too loosely because they feel invincible.

This is the layer of the game that cannot be derived from theory alone. It must be observed in action.

This appendix collects two categories of hands:

- **Historical hands**: real hands played by named professionals, on record, that became famous precisely because they demonstrate a concept in extreme or pure form
- **Archetype situations**: scenario-based hands with no specific attribution, representing entire *classes* of decisions that recur constantly at every level of the game

Together, they serve two purposes. First, they show theory applied — the reader can see exactly which concepts from the core modules are at work in a live situation. Second, and more importantly, they document the patterns of human irrationality that any agent must internalize to compete against real players. Knowing that a pot-odds call is -EV is not enough. An agent must also understand *why* a human makes that call anyway, and what that tendency means for how to play against them.

An agent that only knows the theory will play well against other theory-perfect agents. An agent that has also studied these hands will play well against people.

## Part I — Historical Hands

---

### H1 — The Moneymaker Bluff (WSOP Main Event, 2003)

**Players:** Chris Moneymaker vs Sam Farha
**Stage:** Final table, heads-up
**Suits:** ♠ ♥ ♦ ♣

**The hand:**

Moneymaker holds 5♣ 4♣. The board runs out Q♠ 8♦ 6♦ / 2♦ / 8♠. No flush, no straight. Moneymaker has complete air on the river.

He fires a large bluff on the river — roughly pot-sized. Farha, holding Q♥ 9♥ (top pair, good kicker), folds after an agonizing pause.

**Why this hand is in the appendix:**

This is the most famous bluff in poker history, and it is famous for a reason that goes beyond the cards. Moneymaker was an amateur who qualified online for $86. Farha was a seasoned professional with a read that Moneymaker might be bluffing — he was visibly uncertain. He folded anyway.

The lesson is not "big river bluffs work." The lesson is about **two kinds of human pressure colliding**. Moneymaker bet because he had no other way to win the hand. Farha folded because calling off a large portion of your stack with one pair, heads-up, on a scary board, against an unknown opponent who shows no fear, is one of the hardest things a human being can do at a poker table — even when the logic says call.

Farha was not wrong to fold. He was human. He could not fully separate "this hand looks like a bluff" from "but what if I'm wrong and I lose a massive pot." That gap between knowing and doing is where every agent must learn to operate.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Fold equity, polarized river ranges, the emotional cost of calling, amateur vs professional behavioral patterns

### H2 — Isildur1 vs Antonius (Full Tilt Poker, 2009)

**Players:** Viktor Blom (Isildur1) vs Patrik Antonius  
**Format:** Pot-Limit Omaha, $500/$1,000 blinds  
**Suits:** ♠ ♥ ♦ ♣

**The hand:**

Blom holds A♠ A♦ 2♠ 3♠ on a board of 7♠ 5♠ 4♦ / 8♦. He has top set (trip aces), a nut flush draw in spades, and an open-ended straight draw with the ace. His equity in this spot is approximately 60% or higher depending on Antonius's exact holding.

Antonius holds 6♠ 6♦ 9♠ 8♠. He has a flopped straight (5-6-7-8-9 is not possible, but he has a made 4-5-6-7-8 straight on the turn), with a flush draw of his own.

The pot reaches over $1.3 million. Both players get all the chips in. Antonius hits his hand and wins.

**Why this hand is in the appendix:**

The individual hand result is almost irrelevant. What matters is the context. These two players played hundreds of hours at these stakes over a period of weeks. Blom won and lost millions repeatedly. Neither player adjusted, backed down, or played conservatively. They played as if the money was abstract.

This demonstrates something rare: two players operating so far outside normal human risk-aversion that their play approaches a pure mathematical ideal. They did not tilt in the conventional sense. They tilted toward *action* — a different kind of irrationality where the need for high variance and stimulation overrides bankroll discipline.

For an agent, this hand illustrates that even among elite players, psychological patterns shape decisions. Blom's style was not GTO — it was hyper-aggressive with enormous swings. Antonius was willing to play at any stakes against any opponent. Both were exploitable by a patient, disciplined opponent who refused to play their game. Neither would be.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Pot-Limit Omaha equity (high-equity collisions), variance tolerance, action bias as a psychological pattern, bankroll psychology

### H3 — Dwan vs Antonius — The $1.1M Pot (Full Tilt Poker, 2009)

**Players:** Tom Dwan vs Patrik Antonius
**Format:** No-Limit Hold'em, $500/$1,000 blinds
**Suits:** ♠ ♥ ♦ ♣

**The hand:**

The board is 7♠ 5♦ 4♠ / 5♠. There is already over $580,000 in the pot when Dwan bets the turn. Antonius raises. Dwan moves all-in. Antonius calls.

Dwan holds 6♣ 5♣ (trips fives with a straight draw and backdoor equity). Antonius holds A♠ 2♠ (nut flush draw + gutshot straight draw — a massive combo draw).

Antonius has significant equity — roughly 40–45% — and is not making a mistake by calling. The river is a blank. Dwan wins $1.1 million.

**Why this hand is in the appendix:**

This is a **cooler** — both players are playing correctly. There is no error. And yet one player loses over a million dollars on a single hand where everything they did was right.

This hand exists in the appendix as a permanent reminder that correct decisions do not guarantee correct outcomes. Antonius's call is not a mistake. His equity justified the call. He lost. Dwan's willingness to commit at this depth with trips and a draw is also justified. He won. Neither result validates or invalidates the decision.

For an agent, the risk is the opposite of the human risk. Humans are afraid to call correct calls because they remember the times they lost. An agent that over-indexes on outcome rather than process will behave similarly. The correct behavior is identical whether the result is a win or a loss: evaluate the decision, not the outcome.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Cooler dynamics, EV of correct decisions vs outcome, combo draw equity, deep-stack all-in situations

### H4 — Phil Hellmuth's Emotional Range Compression (Multiple, 1990s–2000s)

**Player:** Phil Hellmuth  
**Format:** Various major tournaments  
**Suits:** ♠ ♥ ♦ ♣

**Note:** This entry is not a single hand. It is a documented behavioral pattern across hundreds of hands.

**The pattern:**

Hellmuth, one of the most decorated tournament players of all time (record 17 WSOP bracelets), is also famous for a specific and exploitable tendency: he narrows his calling range dramatically when he is emotionally invested in a pot. When he believes he is ahead, he calls very wide. When he suspects he is behind, he folds far tighter than GTO would dictate — including folding hands with strong equity.

In numerous documented instances, Hellmuth folds what appear to be correct calls against aggressive opponents, not because the math is wrong but because he cannot emotionally process losing a big pot with a medium-strength hand. Conversely, when he believes someone is bluffing him, he sometimes calls with inadequate equity purely out of refusal to be bluffed.

**Why this pattern is in the appendix:**

Hellmuth is not a weak player. He is one of the greatest tournament players who ever lived. And he has a clear, documented psychological pattern that has cost him enormous value over his career.

The lesson: **even the best humans play through their emotional state**. The range of a human player is not only a function of their cards and position. It is also a function of their current emotional state, their recent history, and their ego. A player who just got bluffed is more likely to call the next large bet regardless of equity. A player who is winning big may play recklessly. A player in pain may fold too much to avoid further loss.

An agent that treats every opponent as a neutral EV machine will misread human ranges. An agent that tracks behavioral signals — aggression changes, timing, bet sizing deviations from baseline — can approximate the emotional state and adjust accordingly.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Emotional range compression, tilt as a strategy modifier, ego-driven calling and folding, exploiting psychological state

## Part II — Archetype Situations

---

### A1 — The Set vs Flush Draw All-In on the Flop

**Setup:**

Player A holds 9♥ 9♦ and raises preflop. Player B calls from the button with 8♥ 7♥.

Flop: 9♠ 6♥ 2♥

Player A has flopped top set. Player B has a flush draw (nine hearts remaining) plus an open-ended straight draw (any 5 or any 10 completes a straight) — a massive combo draw with approximately 15 clean outs.

Player A bets. Player B raises. Player A re-raises all-in. Player B calls.

**Equity at the point of all-in:**

- Player A (set of nines): approximately 54%
- Player B (combo draw): approximately 46%

**Why this situation is in the appendix:**

This is one of the most common all-in confrontations in poker, and it is routinely misplayed by both sides for the wrong reasons.

Player A often assumes they are far ahead. They are not — this is near a coin flip. The emotional experience of flopping top set and facing a re-raise leads many players to assume the opponent is bluffing, drawing to a worse hand, or simply making an error. They get all the chips in expecting to be a large favorite and are shocked to see a near-coin-flip equity distribution.

Player B often feels uncertain about committing all their chips "on a draw." This framing is incorrect. A 15-out combo draw with two cards to come is not a draw hoping to get lucky — it is a legitimate equity position that justifies the all-in call or shove.

The lesson for an agent: **the emotional framing of "made hand vs draw" is misleading**. A 46% equity position is not behind in any meaningful strategic sense. The mathematically correct play — get the chips in — is psychologically uncomfortable for the made hand player and psychologically uncertain for the draw player. Understanding this dissonance helps predict and exploit deviations from correct play.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Combo draw equity, made hand overconfidence, draw underconfidence, flop all-in equity distributions

### A2 — The River Hero Call

**Setup:**

Pot is 400. Player A (in position) has been bet into on every street by Player B (out of position). The river completes a possible flush (3♠ on a board of K♠ 8♠ 2♥ / 5♣ / 3♠).

Player B fires a large river bet — 350, almost the pot.

Player A holds K♦ Q♣ — top pair, second kicker. Player B's range contains:

- Value hands: flushes, sets, two pair
- Bluffs: busted straight draws, missed hands with showdown value who turned to aggression

**The decision:**

Player A must decide whether their top pair is good enough to call a near-pot bet. The pot odds require approximately 47% equity. Whether to call depends entirely on how often Player B is bluffing vs. value betting.

**Why this situation is in the appendix:**

The river hero call is one of the most psychologically loaded decisions in poker. It requires:

1. Correctly estimating the opponent's bluffing frequency
2. Overriding the emotional aversion to "calling with just top pair"
3. Accepting that you will lose the call a significant percentage of the time even when correct

Human players consistently under-call river bets because the experience of calling and losing is more painful than the experience of folding and "saving" chips. This is a well-documented bias — **loss aversion asymmetrically affects calling frequency**. Players fold slightly +EV calls repeatedly because the emotional downside of being shown a value hand outweighs the intellectual understanding that calling is correct.

Conversely, some players hero call too often because they cannot accept being bluffed. The result is the same — a deviation from the correct frequency driven entirely by psychology, not math.

For an agent: the correct call frequency on the river is determined by pot odds and estimated bluff frequency. Nothing else. Tracking whether an opponent has been caught bluffing recently, is showing signs of aggression bias, or has been playing scared will sharpen the bluff frequency estimate — but the underlying call decision should remain purely mathematical.

**Concepts demonstrated:** River bluff catching, loss aversion, pot odds for calls, opponent bluffing frequency estimation

### A3 — The Continuation Bet Into a Calling Station

**Setup:**

Player A raises preflop from the cutoff with A♠ K♦. Player B calls from the big blind — a player with a known tendency to call any bet on any street regardless of strength.

Flop: 7♥ 4♣ 2♦ (completely dry, misses Player A's hand entirely)

Player A c-bets 60% of the pot, continuing their preflop story of strength.

Player B calls.

Turn: J♦ (still no help for Player A)

Player A fires again — 70% of the pot.

Player B calls again.

River: 5♣

Player A now faces a choice: fire a third barrel as a bluff, or give up and check-fold.

**Why this situation is in the appendix:**

This is the classic dilemma of **applying a GTO-derived strategy against a non-GTO opponent**. Standard theory says c-bet on a dry board with a strong preflop range. Against most opponents, this is correct. Against a calling station, it is a guaranteed loss — a calling station does not respond to bet sizing, board texture, or range signals. They call because they call.

The correct adjustment against a calling station is obvious in theory and almost universally ignored in practice: **stop bluffing**. Give up the hand, preserve chips, and wait to bet for value with strong hands that a calling station will pay off.

The reason players fail to make this adjustment is ego. Betting represents agency — it feels like doing something. Checking and folding feels like surrendering. Many players, even experienced ones, continue firing into a calling station because stopping feels like admitting the bluff failed. The result is that the calling station, playing a "bad" strategy of calling everything, extracts profit from the "good" player's inability to exploit them correctly.

For an agent: identifying player type is not optional. A calling station and a GTO-aware player require fundamentally different strategies. The agent that applies the same strategy to all opponents will be exploited by opponents whose deviations are systematic and consistent.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Player type identification, exploitative adjustment, bluff frequency against calling stations, ego-driven betting

### A4 — Tilt Propagation: The Hand After the Bad Beat

**Setup:**

Player A loses a large pot as a 75% favorite — their top set was cracked by a runner-runner flush. They lose 150 BB.

The very next hand, Player A is dealt A♥ K♠ in early position and raises. A player in middle position 3-bets to a standard size. Player A 4-bets all-in.

The 3-bettor calls with Q♥ Q♣.

Player A has approximately 46% equity — a coin flip. But the shove was not motivated by equity. It was motivated by the need to get back to even quickly.

**Why this situation is in the appendix:**

This is **tilt in its purest structural form** — not screaming, not playing every hand, but a single decision made with the wrong decision-making framework. Player A's shove with A-K is not a catastrophic error in isolation. A-K vs Q-Q is a coin flip and a reasonable all-in in many contexts. The error is the *motivation*: getting all-in as fast as possible to recover a loss.

Tilt does not always look like madness. Sometimes it looks like an almost-correct decision made for the wrong reason. The result is a slight but consistent degradation in decision quality — more variance-seeking, less patience, lower threshold for commitment.

The propagation element is important: tilt from one hand contaminates the next hand, and the next. A player who does not recognize and reset between hands accumulates a compounding error rate.

For an agent: there is no emotion, so there is no tilt. This is an advantage. But recognizing tilt in opponents is equally valuable. A player who just took a bad beat is statistically more likely to play wider, shove lighter, and call with insufficient equity on the next several hands. This is a documented behavioral pattern. Increasing aggression against a recently-tilted opponent — particularly in steal and squeeze spots — is exploitative and correct.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Tilt recognition, emotional decision contamination, exploiting tilted opponents, variance-seeking behavior under loss

### A5 — The Slow Play That Backfired

**Setup:**

Player A flops the absolute nuts. Board: A♥ K♦ Q♠. Player A holds J♠ 10♠ — the nut straight.

Player A checks. Player B bets. Player A calls. (Slow playing to induce more action.)

Turn: 10♦. Board: A♥ K♦ Q♠ 10♦.

Player B bets large. Player A raises. Player B shoves all-in.

Player A snap calls.

Player B holds K♥ 10♥ — two pair on the flop that just became a full house (K-K-10-10-A... wait: the board is A-K-Q-10, Player B holds K-10 for two pair Kings and Tens, but no full house yet).

River: K♣. Board: A♥ K♦ Q♠ 10♦ K♣.

Player B now has K-K-K-10-10 — a full house. Player A's straight loses.

**Why this situation is in the appendix:**

Slow playing the nuts is taught as a legitimate strategy — and it is. But this hand illustrates the precise condition under which slow playing turns a massive equity advantage into a disaster: **when the board can give opponents full houses or better**.

On A-K-Q, slow playing a straight gives every two-pair hand a free chance to boat up. K-K, Q-Q, A-A (less likely), A-K, A-Q, K-Q all have draws to full houses. The slow play was emotionally motivated — the satisfaction of trapping — rather than strategically sound given the specific board and opponent range.

The lesson extends beyond this single situation. Many slow plays are motivated by the anticipation of a big trap rather than a clear analysis of whether the board allows opponents to outdraw cheaply. When the board is safe (dry, paired, no realistic draws), slow playing is low-risk. When the board is dynamic and opponents can improve dramatically, slow playing surrenders a massive equity edge for the psychological pleasure of a trap.

For an agent: slow playing should be a calculated frequency decision based on board safety, opponent range, and stack depth — not a default behavior with strong hands. The appeal of trapping is a human emotional preference. It has no place in a decision made purely by expected value.

**Concepts demonstrated:** Slow play risk on dynamic boards, board safety analysis, equity surrender, trap motivation vs EV motivation
